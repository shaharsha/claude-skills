# Why these rules: the evidence

The studies behind the recipe in SKILL.md, for when a rule needs defending or a writer asks "says who?".

## Shorter gets read and answered

- **Rogers and Lasky-Fink** (Harvard; *Writing for Busy Readers*, 2023) ran hundreds of field experiments. A 49-word survey request to 7,002 people got a 4.8% response rate; the 127-word version got 2.7%. Readers judge how much work a reply takes from the length of the message: 29% of the short email's readers expected the survey to take under five minutes, against 15% for the long one. Deleting every other paragraph of a fundraising letter raised donations 16%. Rogers: "We have never found that fewer words is worse."
- **Their six principles**: less is more; make reading easy; design for easy navigation; use enough formatting but no more; tell readers why they should care; make responding easy.
- **Nielsen Norman Group** (Morkes and Nielsen 1997, 51 users): a concise version (about half the words) scored 58% better on usability, a scannable one 47%, an objective one without marketing language 27%, and all three together 124%. 79% of users scanned new pages; 16% read word by word. Promotional language scored worse on almost every measure.

## Plain words and short sentences

- **GOV.UK** writes for a reading age of 9, including for specialists, because by that age the common-word vocabulary that adults read fastest is in place. It flags sentences over 25 words.
- **US Federal Plain Language Guidelines** and OPM: write for your audience, short sections, active voice, "you" for the reader, sentences averaging 15-20 words.
- **Paul Graham, "Write Simply"**: the less energy readers spend on your prose, the more they have for your ideas, and simple writing makes an empty idea obvious, including to you.
- A widely repeated claim that 8-word sentences are fully understood and 43-word ones by under 10% of readers traces to an American Press Institute study that can't be found. Don't cite it.

## Lead with the point

- **BLUF** (bottom line up front) comes from US Army correspondence rules: "the greatest weakness in ineffective writing is that it doesn't quickly transmit a focused message."
- In Rogers' experiments, readers of long emails often never reached a request placed at the end.

## Formatting

- Adding headings to a nine-paragraph newsletter sent to 46,648 people doubled clicks on its lower half without reducing engagement with the top (Rogers).
- Bold tells readers what matters and licenses skipping the rest, so heavy bold defeats itself (Rogers).

## Machine-written text

- Readers who use AI a lot detect machine text very accurately (299 of 300 by majority vote), mostly from vocabulary and sentence templates, and they still caught text rewritten to avoid their own cue list (Russell et al., ACL 2025).
- The "AI vocabulary" is measurable: "delves" rose about 28-fold in PubMed abstracts in 2024, and at least 13.5% of 2024 abstracts show LLM processing (Kobak et al., Science Advances 2025).
