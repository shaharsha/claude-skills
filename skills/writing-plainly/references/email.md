# Work email

Rules specific to email, on top of the recipe in SKILL.md. Evidence grades: **A** peer-reviewed or primary data, **B** company data (correlational), **C** advice only.

## Subject line

- The topic plus the ask or the date: "Contract v3: sign by Thursday", "November revenue: two numbers explained". Readers triage from the subject.
- About 3-6 words. Boomerang found 3-4 words got the most replies (B, 40M emails); no subject at all got 14%.
- Don't cite the Microsoft study (Yang et al. 2017, A) as proof that short subjects win: long subjects there were mostly announcements nobody replies to.
- ACTION / DECISION / FYI prefixes only if the reader's team already uses them (C). For outside readers, say it in words: "Need your OK on the lease".
- Not "Quick question", "Update" or "Following up". Those say nothing.

## Opening

- A short greeting by name ("Hi Dana,"), then the answer or the ask in the first sentence. In the 127-word version of Rogers and Lasky-Fink's email, some readers never reached the request at the end (A).
- Skip "I hope this email finds you well" and similar. Nothing, or one specific line ("Congrats on the close"), works better (C).
- Match the reader's formality. "Hi" and "Hey" did better than "Dear" in Boomerang's data (B, informal mailing lists).

## Body

- 50-125 words for a normal ask. Reply rates peaked there in Boomerang's data (B) and near 40 words in Yang et al. (A); both fell off for long emails. Too short also fails: under about 25 words the reader can't tell what you want.
- Plain words. Emails written at a 3rd-grade reading level got about 53% replies against 39% at college level (B). Plain also helps non-native and non-technical readers.
- Replying to several questions: answer each one in order, reusing the asker's words, answer first and reason second. For three or more questions, quote each one and answer under it.
- No bold or bullets in a short email. In a long one, bold at most the single ask with its date. Bold tells the reader "this is what matters" and licenses skipping the rest (Rogers, A). Headings help only in long emails. Bullets only for real steps or truly parallel items.
- Name attachments in the body, and check they are attached.

## Asks

- One request per email. People asked to do three things were less likely to do any of them (Rogers, A). Hold the second ask for the next email.
- Who must act and by when. Emails containing a request got about twice the reply rate of those without (14.8% vs 7.5%, Yang et al., A).
- Make the reply cheap: yes/no, or two or three options with your recommendation. A one-word "yes" got about 10x the sign-ups of a five-step process (Rogers, B).
- One to three questions, not eight. Emails with 1-3 questions got about 50% more replies than none (B).
- Follow up once, after a few days, in 50-80 words that restate the ask. Reminders help, with diminishing returns (A for surveys; email figures are vendor data, B).

## Tone

- Thanks in one short line. Six sentences of thanks halved responses compared with a brief "thank you", although 93% of people predicted the long version would win (Rogers, A).
- Mildly warm beats neutral; strong negativity hurts (B, noisy sentiment scoring).
- Bad news: the news first, one line of reason, then the next step. A long cushion before the news wastes a busy reader's time (C).
- Apologize once, briefly, and only for a real fault (C).
- No sarcasm or jokes that need tone of voice: readers detect sarcasm in email barely above chance (Epley, via Rogers).

## Closing

- "Thanks" and the name. "Thanks" and "Thanks in advance" did better than "Best" in Boomerang's data (B). Use "Thanks in advance" only for small asks.
- A short signature. No sign-off paragraph that restates the email.

## Hebrew email

- Short and direct is the norm. "היי/שלום [שם]" and "תודה" with people you work with; "שלום רב" and "בברכה" for first contact with officials or senior people (C, language guides).
- The RTL rules in SKILL.md apply: every sentence starts with a Hebrew word; hyphen only.

## Avoid

- Pleasantries before the point; heavy thanks
- The ask buried at the end; several asks at once
- A vague subject
- Bold and bullets that turn two paragraphs into a report
- Emojis, jokes or sarcasm with people you don't know well
- Treating Boomerang's numbers as causal: they show what correlates with replies
